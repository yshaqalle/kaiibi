Weighted average: what actually shipped

The companion to “What stock costs, and in what order to fix it”, which proposed this as step 3. This is the built thing: one arithmetic change inside receive_stock, the two screens whose words it falsified, and the numbers that prove it. Every frame below is the real rendered copy, not a sketch.

1 · Why it had to change

receive_stock line 173 replaced the cost with the newest line's price. The migration's own comment called it “latest wins”:

update public.products set cost_cents = v_cost, updated_at = now() where id = v_product.id;

Buy 200 bags at $14.10 and 10 at $14.90, and all 210 are valued at $14.90. Every subsequent sale's COGS is the most recent price paid, whatever the units actually cost.

That is replacement cost.

IAS 2.25 permits exactly two formulas for interchangeable goods — FIFO and weighted average — and this was neither. IFRS for SMEs §13.18 says the same. So it was not a refinement; it was the difference between a permitted basis and an impermissible one.

Weighted average rather than FIFO because under inflation FIFO draws COGS from the oldest and cheapest stock, raising reported profit and so raising tax. The two are equals under the standard. The FIFO design and plan are merged (#65, #68) and stay available if it is ever wanted.

2 · The arithmetic

The formula is ordinary. What makes it non‑trivial is which quantity it averages against — and there are two ways to get that wrong that both produce a plausible number.

new_cost = round( (old_qty × old_cost + received_qty × received_cost) / (old_qty + received_qty) )

Trap one — the stock is already updated

receive_stock upserts the stock at line 164 and writes the cost at line 173. So by the time the cost is computed, product_location_stock.stock already includes this delivery. Averaging against it counts the received quantity twice and lands between the two costs — wrong in a way nobody would spot. The fix reads the prior quantity before the upsert.

Trap two — the cost is shop-wide, the stock is not

products.cost_cents is one figure per product across the whole shop, but stock lives per location. So the average is taken against sum(stock) over every location. Averaging against one branch's stock would let the same delivery produce a different cost depending on where it landed.

All four answers, separated

The test's quantities are chosen so every candidate is visibly different. 300 units at $10.00, then a delivery of 100 at $20.00:

ResultCostWhat produced it
Correct1250(300×1000 + 100×2000) / 400
Wrong1200Averaged against post‑upsert stock — the delivery counted twice
Wrong2000“Latest wins” — the bug being fixed
Wrong1500A plain mean of the two costs, ignoring quantity

This took two attempts. The first draft used 200 @ 1410 then 10 @ 1490 — where the correct answer and the post‑upsert bug both give 1414, so the most likely implementation error would have sailed straight through a green test.

3 · Inventory — the disclosure

IAS 2.36(a) requires disclosing the cost formula used. With one formula that is a constant string, not a column or a toggle. Inventory is the only screen in the app that shows a stock value — and its old words were not merely undisclosed, they were accurate about an impermissible basis, which made them wrong the moment the arithmetic changed.

Tone stays context, and still carries no action.

The house rule: wrong means the number is wrong until the reader fixes something and must carry an action. There is nothing a shop can do about the costing basis from Inventory, and a wrong with no fix would train people to skip the whole family — including the uncosted warning above it, which does have one.

The shop‑wide clause survived the change.

products.cost_cents is still one column per product with no store dimension, so a delivery at one branch still moves every branch's cost. That is now a consequence of the design rather than an accident: the average is taken shop‑wide precisely so the same delivery cannot produce a different cost depending on where it landed.

4 · Restock — the preview that started lying

This one was not in the plan. The Restock sheet previews the cost write before committing it — “so the preview can say 4.50 → 4.80 before anything is written”. That arrow was true while receive_stock replaced the cost. The moment it averaged, the preview promised a figure the commit does not produce.

Neither product ends up on that figure.

$4.00 → toward $9.00
test
$4.00 → toward $8.50

Stock is valued at weighted average cost, so each delivery moves a product's cost part of the way toward what you paid — by how much depends on how much you already hold. A product with no cost recorded, or none in stock, takes the delivery's price exactly.

Why “toward”, and not the computed average.

The true result needs the shop‑wide quantity at commit time, and reimplementing the weighted average in TypeScript would be a second copy of the formula — free to drift a cent from the one in the migration, on the one screen whose whole job is to promise what is about to happen. An honest direction beats a precise number that can be wrong.

The exception is stated rather than hidden: with no cost recorded, or none in stock, there is nothing to average against and the delivery's price lands exactly — so that row still renders a flat no cost → $9.00.

The clash warning, which was also falsified

A sheet that prices one product two ways used to warn that “the last store to go through is the one that sticks”. Under an average, both are blended into the one column and store order no longer decides the answer — averaging is commutative up to rounding. It now says that instead, and is still worth flagging, because a product deliberately priced two ways in one sheet is usually a mistake in the sheet.

5 · The four edges

Each has a right answer that differs from the obvious one.

CaseAnswerWhy
old_qty = 0New cost = received costAveraging against nothing is a division by zero. The delivery is the whole basis.
old_cost is nullNew cost = received costNull means unknown, not zero. Averaging an unknown as zero would halve the cost of a product somebody simply never priced.
received_cost is nullLeave the cost aloneThe previous behaviour, and correct: a delivery with no stated price is no evidence about cost.
Stock negative or absentTreat old_qty as 0product_location_stock refuses negatives, so this is belt‑and‑braces.
Null is not zero, and the test makes that expensive to get wrong.

The fixture gives a product 40 units on the shelf with no cost recorded, then receives 5 at $8.00. Correct is 800. A coalesce(cost_cents, 0) implementation gives 89 — visibly, damningly different, rather than a near miss that reads as rounding.

6 · How it is proved

A new database check, verify-weighted-average.sql, takes the suite from 17 to 18. Every test step names the mutation that must turn it red — and all four were applied, watched to fail, and reverted. Each produced exactly the figure predicted before it was run:

Mutation appliedReddensReported
Restore v_new_cost := v_cost unconditionallyCheck 22000

The highlighted row is the one the plan's first fixture could not catch. That is why the quantities are what they are.

The existing suite disagreed, and had to move

verify-stock-receipts.sql checks 2 and 2b asserted “latest wins” as the correct behaviour. They were rewritten rather than deleted. Check 2b is the interesting one: it used to pin which of two lines for one product was latest, and that question no longer has meaning. 7 · What did not change

No past margin moves.

costOfGoodsSold() and every item‑margin figure read sale_items.unit_cost_cents, frozen at sale time, never products.cost_cents. This migration writes only the latter. Historical margins cannot move; only sales made after it lands freeze the averaged cost.

The delivery's own price is preserved.

stock_receipt_items.unit_cost_cents keeps what that delivery cost, not the new average. Rewriting it would destroy the only evidence the average was computed from.

Existing costs are not restated.

There is no way to restate them: that needs the quantity and price of every delivery behind the current stock, and stock_receipt_items only reaches back as far as receipts have been recorded — for stock predating the feature, nowhere at all. So the existing figure becomes the opening average and the formula runs from the next delivery forward: applied prospectively, and disclosed rather than discovered.

It converges quickly. Each delivery pulls the average toward the true figure in proportion to its share of stock on hand, so a shop turning its stock every few weeks is on a real average within a month or two.

Nothing else in the database.

No new table, no cost layers, no basis toggle, and no change to complete_sale, save_stock_count, refund_sale_items or transfer_stock. The migration defines exactly one function.

What this unblocks

Phase 2b — posting to the ledger becomes the next work, and it can now post a COGS figure computed on a formula IAS 2 permits. That was the real blocker; it was never FIFO specifically.

One thing still outstanding.

The migration exists locally only. Until it is deployed, the live app still does “latest wins” — and the plan's end‑to‑end check (receive the same product twice, watch the cost land between the two) could not be run in the app for exactly that reason. The arithmetic is proved at the RPC level against the local stack instead.
