The ledger, the statements, and why nothing is posted twice

Accounting gains a chart of accounts, a general journal, a trial balance, an asset register, an audit log, bank transfers, cash bills, and nine reports. This is the record of the one decision the whole thing rests on — and of the four places that decision is enforced, because none of them is the client.

1. The problem a general ledger creates here

A real general ledger posts a balanced journal entry for every event: each sale, each refund, each bill, each pay run. Doing that in this app means one of two things, and both are bad.

Backfill the history

Generate entries for every sale already in the database. They can then drift from the rows they came from — a refund edited, a sale deleted — and nothing reconciles them. A ledger that silently disagrees with the sales list is worse than no ledger.

Start the ledger from today

Two numbers for one question, and no way to say which is right. A shopkeeper who counts the drawer and gets a different figure from the app stops trusting the app — and they are correct to.

The operational tables are already the record of truth and are already good. A sale is the truth about revenue, expenses about spend, cash_accounts about cash, invoices about what is owed. The ledger’s job is not to re-record any of it.

2. Fed or posted, never both

Every account carries a feed: the name of the operational stream it reports, or null. 1000 Cash on hand has feed cash_on_hand, and its balance is the sum of the cash accounts, computed when the statement is drawn. Accounts with no feed — opening equity, a loan, an accrual, a correction — are hand-posted through the general journal.

The rule: a fed account cannot be hand-posted to, and a posted account has no feed.

This is the single way the design could produce a wrong number. Credit 1000 Cash on hand by hand and the shop’s money is counted twice — once by the journal, and once by the drawer the owner went on to re-count.

Where it is enforced

Unique index One account per feed per shop. Two accounts both claiming sales_revenue would each report the full period’s takings, and the trial balance would be out by exactly one period of revenue.
Check constraint A fed account cannot carry an opening balance. The feed already reports everything the account holds, day one included. Same double count, different door.
RPC post_journal_entry refuses a line against a fed account — and refuses an unbalanced entry, a line carrying both a debit and a credit, and an account belonging to another shop.
Client The picker only offers postable accounts (postableAccounts), so the form never proposes something the server will reject. This layer is a courtesy, not the enforcement.

All four are exercised by supabase/tests/verify-ledger.sql, which builds a shop, tries each forbidden thing, and rolls back.

3. What each account reports

Twenty-eight accounts, seeded per shop by a trigger and backfilled for every shop that already existed. Small on purpose: a shop that sells things over a counter needs about twenty accounts, and handing it the two hundred a generic package ships with is how a chart of accounts becomes something nobody maintains. Numbering is the conventional one, so an accountant handed it recognises 1000s as assets and 4000s as income without being told.

Two expense categories deliberately have no account.

Inventory restock is not a cost — it is one asset becoming another, and the goods it bought are already counted by the inventory feed. Giving restock spend an account would count the same goods twice.

Owner’s draw is not a cost of trading. It reduces equity, through the contra account of the same name.

Both already sit outside the P&L’s operating subtotal (NON_OPERATING_CATEGORIES). This is the same rule stated in the ledger’s vocabulary, and the two must agree.

4. Three statements, one set of balances

accountBalances() produces one array; the income statement, the balance sheet and the trial balance are three arrangements of it. The balance sheet computes retained earnings by calling the income statement rather than re-summing income and expense accounts, so the two reports cannot drift apart. A test pins that.

The contra handling is the part that goes wrong

A contra account is filed under one type and leans the other way: accumulated depreciation is an asset that carries a credit, an owner’s draw is equity that carries a debit. Judged by type alone, accumulated depreciation lands in the debit column and the trial balance is out by twice the depreciation charged — which sends someone hunting for an arithmetic slip that is not there. Hence accountNormalBalance(), which reads the contra flag, and the tests that pin both directions.

A difference is shown, never plugged

In a hand-kept ledger, debits not meeting credits means an arithmetic mistake. Here it almost always means the shop has not yet said what it started with — the capital the owner put in, and any profit kept from before these books began. The trial balance and the balance sheet both name that, and offer the figure an opening entry would need to be. Neither applies it: that is a decision about the business, and an app that quietly closed the gap would be making it on the owner’s behalf and hiding that it had.

5. What “as of” means, and why it is not a fudge

The screen produces a period-end picture, which is what a period-end trial balance is:

Right now Cash, receivables, inventory, assets, payables. A cash account holds a single confirmed balance and stock holds a single count — there is no history behind either, so “inventory on 31 March” is not a question this app can answer. Inventing an answer would be worse than saying so, and the balance sheet says so.
The period Income and expenses. These follow the range picker, as they should.
All time Posted journal movement. A loan taken out last year is still owed; scoping the journal to the range would report it as repaid.

Sales tax payable is the period’s, not a running total

The app records tax collected but not tax remitted, so a running total would climb forever and describe a debt no shop has. What a filing needs is the period’s figure, and that is what the account carries.

6. Depreciation is computed, never posted

There is no monthly job writing entries and no accumulated_depreciation_cents column. An asset’s depreciation at any date is a function of its cost, its life and the date — straight line, cost less salvage, counted in whole months from the month after acquisition, rounded once at the end.

A missed month cannot leave the books wrong, because there is no month to miss.

And a stored figure that disagrees with the formula cannot exist. The cost is that every statement recomputes it — which is why asset-depreciation.ts is pure and has the tightest tests in Accounting.

Why rounding once matters

Rounding a monthly figure and multiplying it leaves the final month short by up to a cent per month of life. On a five-year asset that is a 60-cent hole in the balance sheet that nothing else will ever explain. A test pins that three consecutive periods of a 100-cent, 3-month asset sum to exactly 100.

A disposal posts nothing either

The moment an asset is marked disposed it stops being reported, on the cost line and the accumulated-depreciation line together, in step. The difference between what it fetched and what it was still worth becomes the period’s gain or loss through the asset_disposal_result feed. A journal entry as well would count the removal twice. The same argument applies to a cash transfer, which also posts nothing: both balances move, both feeds see it, and the shop still has exactly as much money as before.

7. Four things the database will not let you do

Each of these is enforced in Postgres rather than in the app, because the app is not what protects the books. Every one is exercised by verify-ledger.sql.

Unbalance An entry balances or it does not exist. Validated before anything is written, in one transaction, so a rejected entry leaves no header behind for someone to find later and wonder about.
Edit A posted entry is reversed, never edited. A second entry, dated when the correction was made, every line the other way round. Not ceremony: an edited entry silently changes a period somebody has already reported on. And a reversal happens once — a unique index, so a double tap cannot undo the same entry twice and leave the books out by its own amount.
Tamper The audit log is append-only, for everyone. No insert, update or delete policy — not for a manager, not for an owner. Entries are written by triggers, so a log written by the client would be a log with a hole in it exactly where somebody bypassed the client. Each entry freezes its own summary and the actor’s name, so it outlives the row it describes.
Orphan An account with journal lines against it cannot be deleted (restrict), and a seeded account cannot be deleted at all — a feed with no account to report it would vanish from the balance sheet silently. Archiving is what the chart offers instead.
One bug this caught before it shipped.

The audit triggers fire during the cascade when a shop is deleted — and Postgres removes the parent row before processing the cascade, so the log entry had no shop to point at and the foreign key rejected it. That did not just lose a log entry: it aborted the delete entirely. verify-owner-membership.sql found it on the first run. write_accounting_audit now returns early when the shop is already gone, which costs nothing — the log cascades on the same key, so any entry written would have been deleted a moment later.

8. Where it all lands on screen

One new tab, five sub-views

Accounting’s pill row already carried eight destinations. Thirteen is not a row anybody scans — it is a thing you scroll sideways looking for something you half remember the name of. The five bookkeeping surfaces belong together anyway: they are what a bookkeeper opens, and everything else on the screen is what a shopkeeper opens.

Nine reports behind a picker

Somebody looking for “balance sheet” is looking for a document, not a section of the app, so the picker names documents. Each report owns its own fetch and only the one on screen runs it — which matters here more than anywhere, because the statements each pull the whole ledger.

Profit & loss and Income statement are two entries rendering one component. They are the same document under two names, and both names are in the picker because both are what somebody went looking for. Giving them separate reports would mean two profit figures for one period — exactly the drift lib/pnl.ts was written to prevent.

The one place two profit figures legitimately differ

The Sales report’s profit is the trading view, built from money in and money out; it is what the Dashboard and Overview show. The income statement adds depreciation, which is real and non-cash. Where the two differ, the income statement says so, with the amount, in a context caveat — the number is right, and here is why it looks surprising.

Cash bills

Every bill used to be a credit bill. The commoner case — a supplier hands over goods, is paid on the spot, hands over a receipt — had two bad options: log it as an expense and lose the invoice number, the vendor and the trail; or log it as a bill and have the shop look as though it owes money it has already handed over. A bill now says which it is, and a cash bill is raised and settled in one transaction through record_bill — because an insert followed by a separate payment call can half-succeed, and what that leaves behind is exactly the wrong picture.

9. What is deliberately not here

Not builtWhy
Automatic posting from sales, expenses, bills and pay runs Section 1. The feeds are the answer; entries would be a second set of numbers to reconcile.
An indirect cash-flow statement It needs each balance-sheet figure at both ends of the period, and there is no history to read the opening figures from. The direct method needs none — every line is a sum over rows carrying the date they happened on.
A year-end close that rolls profit into retained earnings A shop that skipped one would find its balance sheet quietly wrong ever after. The period’s profit is computed instead, on top of an opening figure somebody typed once.
Reducing-balance and units-of-production depreciation Real methods a shop this size does not use. Each extra method is another set of figures nobody can check by hand.
Historical inventory and cash balances Would make a true as-of balance sheet possible. It needs a snapshot table and a job that writes it — its own piece of work, with its own failure modes.
Tax remittance Without it, sales tax payable can only honestly report the period’s figure rather than a running liability.
