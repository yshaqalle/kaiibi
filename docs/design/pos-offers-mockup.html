Offers at the counter, and what the sale remembers

Promotions already come off automatically at the till — and the sale keeps only the cents, never which offer produced them. This is the other half of the Marketing tab: the cashier side. Four views, in the order a sale actually goes: the discount lands by itself, the customer's own campaign offer gets one prompt, an offer that needs a decision gets a picker, and the finished sale keeps a name that survives the promotion being expired, renamed or deleted.

The rule the whole design turns on

The cashier picks which offer. Never how much. Selecting an offer reads discount_type and discount_value off the promotion row and hands them to the same discountAmountCents() that auto-apply already uses — capped so a line can never go negative. There is no number to type and no arithmetic to get wrong.

Today the only manual path is the opposite of that: discount-editor.tsx asks the cashier to type a percentage or a cash amount, and permissions.ts has no discount permission at all — so any cashier can take any amount off any sale and nothing records why. Offers as selectable objects are what makes "may apply offers, may not type a discount" expressible for the first time.

Auto-apply keeps working, untouched

A store-wide 20% must not depend on a busy cashier remembering to select it. Forget it once and the customer is overcharged — worse than any missing attribution. bestPromotionForProduct() stays exactly as it is; the cart just labels its result instead of showing an anonymous strike-through.

The picker only holds things that need deciding

A new auto_apply flag splits the table in two. False means the offer never fires on its own and lives in "Apply an offer" — campaign codes, staff discount, a manager's goodwill gesture. A dropdown of every promotion in the shop would be a list nobody reads.

One prompt beats a list

When a customer is attached to the cart and Marketing sent them an offer, the till says so in one line with one button. That single interaction is also the cleanest attribution in the system: sale → campaign → recipient, no inference.

The window is new, and it matters more than the picker

promotions has no starts_at and no ends_at — only an active boolean somebody has to remember to switch off. A weekend sale left running until September is the current default behaviour, and a sale that must open at Thursday's opening hour has to be switched on by hand at Thursday's opening hour. The window fixes both.

The promotion window

Two nullable timestamps on promotions, and the offer runs itself. Most of what shops sell against is short-lived — a weekend, the three days before Eid, a Thursday evening — and the current design makes every one of those a thing a human has to switch on and then remember to switch off.

starts_atNull means "already running". Set it and the promotion appears in the shop's list as Scheduled, applies nothing, and opens itself at the minute given — so Thursday's sale can be built on Tuesday.
ends_atNull means "until I turn it off" — the current behaviour, still available for a standing loyalty discount. Set it and the promotion stops applying on its own, with no cleanup and no forgotten discount running into next month.
active staysIt is the hard "off now" switch, unchanged. The window is scheduling; active = false is an owner overriding it. A promotion applies only when it is active and inside its window.
One filter, one placebestPromotionForProduct() already filters on active and is the single gate every line goes through — the window is two more comparisons in that same predicate, so the cart, the product tile badge and the total can never disagree about whether an offer is live.
Where the dates surfaceThe picker greys an expired or unstarted offer and shows why. Marketing's {ends} token reads ends_at instead of the owner typing a date into message text that then goes stale. The poster prints it.

The one thing the window does not do is reach backwards. A sale rung up while the promotion was live keeps its discount forever — that is what the frozen columns below are for.

What the sale stores

Three columns, and the pattern is one this schema already uses: sale_items keeps product_name beside product_id precisely so deleting a product cannot wreck a receipt from last year. Offers get the same treatment.

promotion_idNullable FK, on delete set null. Groups the reports. Worst case the link goes; the sale never breaks.
promotion_nameText, frozen at checkout. The receipt and the report still read "Eid weekend — 20% off" after the promotion is expired, renamed, archived or deleted.
discount_centsAlready exists, already frozen. Editing the promotion from 20% to 15% must never rewrite what a customer paid last week.
campaign_id on salesSet only by the customer prompt. This is the column that turns "bought within 7 days" from a correlation into a redemption somebody actually claimed.
Deleting a used promotionArchives instead. Hard delete stays available for one created by mistake — an owner should not be told "you cannot remove this, it was used 400 times", and equally should not be able to blank 400 rows of history by accident.

Nothing above changes how a sale is totalled. complete_sale keeps receiving the line's discount_cents exactly as it does today — the new fields ride alongside as a record of where that figure came from.

Open questions

  • Should the customer prompt auto-apply, or always ask? Drawn here as asking. It is one tap, and a cashier who did not know an offer existed learns something. Auto-applying would be faster and would remove the only place a campaign redemption can be lost.
  • Can an offer be applied to a sale with no customer? Drawn as yes — a customer who walks in quoting the message but is not in the directory should still get their discount. The attribution is weaker, and the report says "claimed, no customer attached" rather than pretending otherwise.
  • Does the free-form discount need the permission now, or later? The gap is real today and independent of any of this. It could ship first, on its own.
