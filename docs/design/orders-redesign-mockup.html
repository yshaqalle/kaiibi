Orders: the table it should have been, and the three things it still can't do

The screen works — the state machine behind it is airtight — but it is one chip row and one bare table, and a shop that is short on stock has exactly one move: cancel the whole order. This covers the redesign first, then the three capabilities behind it — amending an order, filling part of one, and a link the customer can open — which turn out to be one feature wearing three hats.

The through-line: a shop cannot fill an order in full, so it amends it down, sends the customer a link showing exactly what changed, settles the rest on WhatsApp, and carries on. Every piece below exists to serve that one sentence.

Three things the current screen can't say

Found while reading the migrations. Each one shows up in the redesign.

1
A completed order reaches Transactions for less than the customer paid. complete_storefront_order passes subtotal_cents — goods only — to complete_sale, because the fee would be rejected as an over-payment. The delivery fee is posted separately to 4300 Delivery Income. A $47.50 order appears as a $44.50 sale. The books are right; the row is confusing. shown on the row
2
Nothing marks a sale as having come from an order. The link runs one way only — orders.sale_id → sales. There is no sales.order_id. Once completed, an online order is indistinguishable from a walk-in. reconciliation block in the sheet
3
Editing an order isn't currently possible at all. 20260928000300_orders_write_lockdown.sql revoked insert, update, delete on orders from authenticated outright. Every legal move goes through an RPC. Changing a customer's phone or a delivery landmark needs a new security definer function — it is not a UI-only change. needs a migration

1
The stat strip earns the space by saying something the table can't. "Needs you now" is a count of orders nobody has looked at, with the oldest one's age — that is the only number on this screen a shop must act on within the hour. "Promised" is deliberately not called revenue, and the caveat under it repeats why in words.
2
Waiting is Orders' own column. A sale is instantaneous, so Transactions has no equivalent and never needed one. An order that has sat unaccepted for four hours is the failure mode this screen exists to prevent, so it gets a sortable column and an amber step past a threshold — with the digits, never colour alone.
3
One inline button, and only ever the legal one. Read from the same canAccept / canMarkReady / canComplete rules the sheet already uses, which are read off the permitted-moves table in 20260928000100_order_transitions.sql. Terminal rows get an em dash, not a disabled button. Cancel is never inline — it needs a typed reason, so it stays in the sheet.
4
The delivery split is stated on the row. incl. $3.00 delivery under the total, and on a completed order the sale figure it actually became. Finding 1, answered where the confusion happens.
5
Shortfalls surface before you open anything. checkOrderFulfilment already runs on open; running it for the visible open rows puts "short 2" in the Items column, so a shop sees what it cannot fill while scanning rather than one order at a time.

1
The stage rail is the answer to "move it to the different stages". A word in a badge tells you where an order is; the rail tells you where it has been, how long each step took, and what is left. Cancelled renders as a fifth, red terminal step replacing whatever was next — an off-ramp, not a stop on the road.
2
Edit is drawn, and it is the one part that isn't free. Customer and fulfilment are the two fields worth correcting — a mistyped phone number or a landmark the driver can't find. Both need a new security definer RPC; authenticated has no UPDATE on orders. Line items are deliberately not editable: changing them changes the total the customer agreed to at checkout, and complete_storefront_order already refuses an order whose price has moved (order_total_changed).
3
The reconciliation block closes finding 1 and 2 together. It names the sale the order became and the account the fee went to, so a shop reconciling by hand stops looking for $3.00 that was never missing. It renders only for a completed order — there is nothing to reconcile before that.
4
Everything else in the sheet — the shortfall lines, the Goods / Delivery / Total breakdown, the cancellation-reason form, the POS-access note — is what order-detail.tsx already renders today, unchanged.

1
Three buttons that each say what they do. A single "partial fulfilment" action with a keep-the-rest toggle is a thing people get wrong at speed. Today only Cancel order exists, which is why a shortfall is currently a dead end.
2
The re-price is stated, never silent. complete_sale prices from today's products.price_cents and ignores the checkout snapshot — so an amend that keeps the old figure builds an order that can never complete (order_total_changed). Recomputing is mandatory; showing the delta is what makes it honest, and the customer's link is where it gets agreed.
3
Two reasons, and only one of them travels. "Out of sugar; Amina agreed on the phone" is written for the shop weeks later — the same job cancellation_reason already does, and real ones are blunt. It must never reach a forwarded URL. The customer-facing line is separate, optional, and written to be read.
4
"Awaiting customer" is a flag, not a sixth status. A new word in the vocabulary would mean touching the status CHECK, the permitted-moves table, ORDERS_NEEDING_ACTION, the tabs and the badge map — for something orthogonal to where the order actually is. It rides alongside the stage instead.
5
It warns, it does not block. #1040's "Mark ready" is still live, just no longer the filled button. A shop that got a verbal yes must not be locked out because the customer never tapped anything — that only teaches people to route around the feature.

1
This is the feature that pays for itself. Most opens will be the third frame — a customer asking "where is my order?" without phoning. The amendment case is the reason to build it; the status case is the reason it gets used.
2
Yes writes; no doesn't. "Yes, that's fine" calls an anon RPC that stamps customer_confirmed_at and nothing else. "Something's wrong" writes nothing — it opens WhatsApp. A link that has been forwarded, screenshotted or leaked must never be able to cancel or alter an order, so the only anon write is one that agrees with what the shop already proposed.
3
The token is random, never the order number. assign_order_number is sequential per shop — #1042 tells you #1041 exists. A guessable URL would hand a stranger a name, a phone number and a delivery landmark.
4
A fifth anon RPC, added deliberately. 71d0dcd cut the anon surface from 74 functions to 4 on purpose, and the survivors are exactly this family — the public storefront. get_public_order(token) belongs to it, but it should arrive with the same argued header the other four carry, returning only what is drawn here: stage, lines, totals, the customer-facing note. No cost prices, no stock levels, no shortfall counts, no internal reason, no sale id.
5
Links expire. A forwarded URL lives forever otherwise. Some window after the order reaches a terminal state, plus a regenerate action for when a shop knows one went astray — and the RPC answers "not found" identically for a bad token and an expired one.

The loop, end to end

What the shop does, what the customer does, and which of it the app is responsible for.

StepWhere it happensWhat the app records
Customer places the orderStorefrontplace_storefront_order — now also mints the share token and returns it
Shop acceptsOrders row, inlinetransition_order, unchanged
Shelf is short, or the customer changes their mindAmend sheetamend_order — new lines, recomputed total, internal reason, customer note, amended_at
Shop sends the linkWhatsApp, one tapNothing — the link is stable and already exists
Customer reads the diff, repliesThe link, then WhatsAppconfirm_public_order on yes. Nothing on no.
Shop carries onOrders row, inlinetransition_order → ready → complete_storefront_order

The one thing the app deliberately does not do is broker the agreement. It records what the shop proposed, shows the customer exactly that, and notes whether they said yes. The negotiation itself stays on WhatsApp, which is where the order arrived from in the first place (placeOrderViaWhatsApp).

Four things in that table are real questions rather than work. The staleness threshold — what counts as "too long", and does it differ for collect vs deliver. Whether the rail shows times at all — no per-transition history is stored today, so honest times mean new columns and a trigger, and the cheap version is sequence with no clock. How long a share link lives after an order finishes. And whether an amend may add a product the customer never ordered — drawn here as allowed, but "we substituted something" is a different conversation from "we sent you less", and it may deserve to be refused outright rather than left to a shop's judgement.
